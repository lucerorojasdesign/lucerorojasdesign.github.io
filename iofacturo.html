Overview

From scattered solutions to a shared product experience.

When I joined GoSocket, electronic invoicing already existed across several Latin American markets but not as a unified product.

The experience was distributed across country-specific widgets embedded within a larger portal. As the business evolved, new functionality had often been added to existing solutions independently, creating differences in structure, navigation, and interface patterns.

At the same time, the company was moving toward a new technical architecture based on microservices. This created an opportunity to rethink the invoicing experience and build a product with a clearer structure, shared design foundations, and the flexibility to support different market requirements.

The starting point

A product built in pieces.

Electronic invoicing functionality already existed, but it lived as separate country-specific experiences within a larger portal.

Depending on the selected country, users could encounter different interfaces and patterns. Functionality had evolved over time through individual additions and market-specific solutions rather than through a shared product structure.

The challenge was not simply to redesign a set of screens. It was to understand what already existed, identify what should remain, and create a more cohesive foundation for the product moving forward.

The goal was not to make every market identical, but to create a shared foundation while preserving the flexibility required by different business and regulatory contexts.

The opportunity

More than a redesign.

The new invoicing product was being shaped while the broader platform was also evolving. Three areas were changing at the same time.

Technology

Information architecture

Turning existing functionality into a product structure.

The existing solutions contained functionality that had been added over time across different country-specific experiences. My first challenge was to map what already existed and determine how those capabilities should live within the new product.

I created an initial information architecture that brought together the functionality from the existing solutions. I then worked with the Product Owner to evaluate what should remain, how features should be grouped, and whether they still made sense within the new experience.

Competitive research and emerging product patterns also informed decisions around navigation, hierarchy, and ways to make the product easier to understand.

The goal wasn't to preserve every existing feature exactly as it was. It was to determine what still belonged in the product and how it could work as part of a more coherent system.

Designing the experience

Turning complexity into structure.

Three areas best represent how I approached the product experience: simplifying complex tax documents, creating a clearer structure for customers and products, and designing a product overview that helped users understand what was happening across the platform.

01 - Tax documents

Not all complexity belongs in the interface.

Creating tax documents was one of the most complex areas of the product.

The interface needed to support extensive regulatory requirements and business rules, but presenting every possible field to the user would result in an overwhelming and difficult-to-use form.

The challenge was to determine what information users truly needed to provide, and what complexity the system could handle in the background.

Asking for only what the user needs to provide.

I reviewed the available business rules and worked to distinguish between information that needed to come directly from the user and data that could be retrieved or completed by the system.

When information could be obtained from another source and used to generate the required XML, I explored ways to avoid asking users to enter it again.

The goal was not to remove necessary complexity, but to prevent users from having to manage complexity the system could handle for them.

User input

Only information the user needs to provide.

System data

Information retrieved or completed automatically.

Translating business rules into interface behavior.

Many requirements were documented as business rules, including conditions that determined when certain fields were required, optional, visible, or hidden.

While those rules existed in spreadsheets, I needed to translate them into visual behavior that was easier to understand and implement.

Creating consistency across different document types.

Different tax documents required different information, but I looked for opportunities to maintain a familiar structure wherever possible.

Consistent layouts, labeling, field patterns, and interaction behavior helped reduce unnecessary variation while still allowing the experience to support document-specific requirements.

The challenge was not to make every document identical. It was to create a familiar experience while supporting the differences that actually mattered.

02 - Customers & products

Giving scattered information a clear home.

Customers and products were important parts of the invoicing workflow, but the existing functionality was dispersed rather than organized as dedicated product areas.

As part of the new structure, I designed dedicated catalog experiences that made it easier to view, manage, and add records.

Reusing structure instead of redesigning the same problem twice.

Customer and product catalogs contained different information, but shared interaction needs. I used reusable patterns for tables, hierarchy, and actions while adapting each catalog to its content.

I also considered which information users needed to see first and how to make the primary action clearly stand out from the surrounding content.

Information hierarchyReusable table patternsPrimary CTAShared interaction structure

Similar needs were supported through shared patterns, creating a more consistent experience and reducing unnecessary variation.

Design system

Turning consistency into a product capability.

To support expansion across markets, I led the creation of a modular design system that aligned visual language, interaction patterns, and reusable components. It gave teams a shared foundation while allowing country-specific requirements to be incorporated without rebuilding the experience.

From brand colors to a product color system.

I started with the primary and secondary colors defined by Marketing and expanded them into scales for product interfaces. I also introduced neutral and semantic colors for text, error, success, and warning states, creating a palette that could support more than the original brand applications.

Creating a hierarchy for product interfaces.

The typeface had already been selected as part of the brand. I translated it into a consistent hierarchy for headings, titles, body text, and supporting content, helping establish clearer structure across product screens and flows.

Growing the component library with the product.

Rather than defining every possible component before designing the product, I started with the patterns it needed most. As new flows introduced new requirements, I evaluated which solutions could be reused and added or updated components accordingly.

  1. Product need
  2. UI pattern
  3. Reusability check
  4. Create or update component
  5. Reuse

Designing for states, errors, and alternative scenarios.

Each flow considered more than the ideal path. I designed interfaces for errors, empty states, conditional behavior, and alternative scenarios. This was especially important when creating tax documents, where different inputs and business rules could change what users needed to see or do next.

Evolving with the product and the tools.

The first version of the system used the Figma capabilities available at the time. As the product evolved and Figma introduced variables, I migrated the color foundations into variables and created tokens that made the system easier to maintain. This also supported light and dark themes.

Improving the visual direction and accessibility.

As the product matured, I proposed an updated color palette that felt more contemporary and supported better color contrast. I used accessibility plugins to evaluate combinations, then updated the foundations, components, and product screens to reflect the new visual direction.

Designing beyond desktop.

The design foundations included layout grids and responsive considerations as the product expanded across screen sizes. I explored how layouts and components could adapt to desktop, tablet, and mobile, beyond the primary desktop experience.

Core experience

Making invoicing feel guided, not technical.

The redesign translated complex regulatory requirements into a clearer sequence of decisions. Rather than exposing every field at once, the experience guides users through the information needed to create and issue an invoice with greater confidence.

Guided invoicing

A clearer path from setup to issuance.

The flow organizes information progressively, clarifies required actions, and provides feedback at the moments where users are most likely to hesitate or make errors.

Reflection

What this project taught me about designing for scale.

Working on ioFacturo taught me that scalability does not come from making everything identical.

Designing for multiple markets required understanding what needed to remain consistent, where flexibility was necessary, and how to build foundations that could evolve as the product changed.

It also reinforced the importance of deciding where complexity belongs. Users should not have to manage every business rule, technical dependency, or regulatory requirement when the product can handle part of that complexity for them.

For me, the project became an exercise in turning complexity into structure, at the product, interface, and system levels.
