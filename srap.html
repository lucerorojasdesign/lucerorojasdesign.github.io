The opportunity

A global team brought together to solve a local challenge.

SRAP (Synoptic Cancer Reporting for Anatomical Pathology) was developed as part of an IBM global pro bono initiative that brings together selected IBM professionals to work on high-impact challenges.

After a competitive selection process, I was selected to join an international, multidisciplinary team working with INCAN, Paraguay's National Cancer Institute.

Our team included professionals from different countries and backgrounds, including the United States, Australia, India, Colombia, Europe, and Mexico. Before meeting in person, we spent two months collaborating remotely, getting to know one another, understanding the organization, and preparing for the work ahead.

We then traveled to Paraguay to work directly with INCAN and develop a proof of concept grounded in the realities of its healthcare environment.

The context

When critical information depends on manual processes.

Before designing a solution, we needed to understand how cancer reporting actually happened.

At INCAN, key parts of reporting and record management relied heavily on manual and paper-based processes. Synoptic reporting organizes pathology findings into a standardized, structured format, rather than free-form narrative text. Without a unified digital experience, information could be difficult to access or interpret, creating opportunities for inconsistency and inefficiency.

Why it matters

In cancer care, information delays can have real consequences.

Cancer reporting is part of a broader chain of decisions and care. When critical information takes longer to create, access, interpret, or share, it can add friction to processes where timely information matters.

While SRAP was a proof of concept and did not measure clinical outcomes, the project explored how more structured and accessible reporting could help support a more efficient flow of information.

At the same time, introducing technology into an existing healthcare environment came with its own challenge: the goal could not simply be to digitize everything.

Any solution needed to support existing professionals and workflows without creating additional complexity.

When the information matters, time matters too.

Better reporting was not just about replacing paper with a screen. It was about exploring how critical information could move through the system with more structure and less unnecessary friction.

The challenge

How might we introduce standardized cancer reporting without making an already complex workflow harder to use?

The project aimed to explore how digital reporting could improve consistency and accessibility while remaining practical for pathologists, doctors, and administrative staff.

The challenge was not simply to create a digital form.

We needed to understand what information mattered, how reports were created, where friction existed, and how a new system could introduce more structure without forcing users to adopt an unnecessarily complicated workflow.

Standardization only works if it fits the way people actually work.

Research

Learning from the people inside the process.

Our research focused on understanding the workflows and priorities of the people involved in cancer reporting, identifying friction in existing processes, and exploring what a more unified digital experience would need to support.

Key insights

The problem was bigger than putting a paper report on a screen.

01 - Lack of consistency

Reporting required significant manual effort.

Pathologists reported spending an average of 2-4 hours per report, partly due to the lack of standardized templates and the need for manual adjustments.

02 - Outdated tools

Existing processes required too much manual work.

70% of respondents expressed dissatisfaction with outdated tools that required frequent manual adjustments.

03 - Practical structure

Users wanted support without complexity.

The most requested capabilities included real-time guideline updates, multi-language support, and a simplified interface.

The opportunity wasn't simply to digitize the report.

It was to create a structured experience that could improve consistency and accessibility while remaining practical for the people doing the work.

Design principle

Don't digitize the complexity.

A digital version of the existing process could easily have reproduced the same complexity in a different format.

Instead, the design focused on understanding which parts of the workflow needed to be preserved, which information needed more structure, and where the experience could reduce unnecessary effort.

Principle 01

Preserve what works.

Respect the knowledge and workflow of the professionals already doing the work.

Principle 02

Structure what matters.

Organize critical information around standardized reporting requirements.

Principle 03

Simplify where possible.

Use the digital experience to reduce unnecessary friction rather than adding more steps.

The proof of concept

Designing the minimum experience needed to make the idea real.

The goal was not to design a complete healthcare platform.

The proof of concept focused on the core experience required to demonstrate how structured cancer reporting could work digitally and provide a foundation that could potentially evolve over time.

Deep dive

Designing structure without overwhelming the user.

The reporting experience was the core of the proof of concept.

My role was to determine how the required information could be presented digitally in a way that supported structured reporting without turning the experience into an overwhelming collection of fields.

I focused on information organization, hierarchy, and interaction patterns that could make a complex process easier to navigate.

01

Keeping patient context visible

Key patient and biopsy details stay at the top while the report is completed.

02

Breaking the report into sections

Synoptic sections expand one at a time and show completion status, so the report doesn't feel like one long form.

03

Bringing approval into the report

Approval, printing, sharing, and comments live alongside the report, replacing manual steps outside the system.

Design system

Using an existing system to focus on the actual problem.

I used IBM Carbon Design System as the visual foundation for the proof of concept.

Rather than creating a new visual language from scratch, using an established design system allowed the design effort to focus on the workflow, information architecture, interactions, and usability of the experience.

The challenge was not to reinvent the visual language. It was to understand the workflow and make the right UX decisions.

Reflection

Designing for adoption means designing for context.

SRAP taught me that entering a new domain does not mean having all the answers before you start.

As the only UX Designer on the team, I had to quickly learn a complex healthcare environment, understand unfamiliar terminology and workflows, and collaborate with people whose expertise was far beyond my own.

The experience reinforced the importance of spending time where the product will actually live. Documentation gave us context, but observing the environment and talking directly with the people involved changed how we understood the problem.

It also taught me that introducing technology is not automatically an improvement. A solution can follow standards, include the right features, and still fail if it does not fit into the reality of the people expected to use it.

In the end, the work was about learning enough to ask better questions, understand what mattered, and translate that complexity into an experience people could realistically use.
